> A large-scale change (LSC) is any set of logically related changes that cannot practically be submitted as one atomic commit — and the largest possible atomic change *shrinks* as a codebase and organization grow. Google's answer is to industrialize refactoring: machines generate the edits, infrastructure (Kythe, Rosie, the TAP train) shards, tests, mails, and submits them, and culture accepts that local owners don't hold a veto. The payoff: sweeping migrations like replacing 500,000+ uses of `scoped_ptr`, plus the freedom to fill small potholes — "some design decisions no longer need to be as fixed as they once were."

## The big idea

The chapter opens with questions every engineer should ask of their own codebase: how many files can you reliably update in a single simultaneous commit, how would you test and review it, could you roll it back? At Google the observation is that as the codebase and headcount grow, the largest feasible atomic change counterintuitively *decreases* — presubmit checks and test runs become unwieldy, and files drift mid-change. Since Google still wants to continually improve its infrastructure, it developed new ways of reasoning about and executing large-scale changes.

An LSC is "any set of changes that are logically related but cannot practically be submitted as a single atomic unit" — the industrial-scale answer to the questions of modifying existing code that Ousterhout raises at the module level. They are almost always generated by automated tooling and fall into recurring categories: cleaning up common antipatterns, replacing deprecated features, enabling infrastructure improvements (compiler upgrades), and moving users from old systems to new ones. Most LSCs have near-zero functional impact — widespread textual updates for clarity, optimization, or future compatibility — and the largest have touched millions of references. The enabling investment is both technical (see the sections below) and social: centralized ownership of migrations instead of unfunded mandates, and a shared belief that machine-generated changes can be trusted.

## Section by section

### 22.1 What Is a Large-Scale Change?

LSCs are defined by practical impossibility of atomicity — too many files for the tooling, or guaranteed merge conflicts, or a repository topology (federated repos) that makes atomicity technically impossible. They're generated by tooling for reasons like a new language standard, a changed library interface, or a new compiler flagging existing problems. At Google's scale, infrastructure teams routinely change hundreds of thousands of references; a footnote notes the largest series of LSCs ever executed deleted **one billion lines of code in three days**. Efficient tooling also scales *down* — the same machinery serves a few dozen related edits.

### 22.2 Who Deals with LSCs?

Infrastructure teams run LSCs, and centralization is a deliberate choice:

- **Domain knowledge** lives with the teams that built the systems; expecting every consumer team to relearn the migration is globally inefficient. Centralization also enables fast error recovery via playbooks, since errors fall into a small set of categories.
- **Unfunded mandates don't work.** Even a categorically better system has diffuse benefits and concentrated migration costs, so individual teams won't migrate on their own initiative. Funding a central team is "internalizing the externalities that an unfunded mandate creates," with economies of scale.
- **Incentives.** Organic migrations rarely fully succeed — engineers copy existing code as examples when writing new code. A team with a vested interest in *removing* the old system ensures the job finishes.

**Case study: filling potholes.** Google's early C++ had `stl_util.h` next to `map-util.h` (inconsistent separators). Purely cosmetic, but mature LSC tooling fixed it in a couple of weeks of background-task effort and measurably reduced build failures. A mature LSC capability also changes decision-making: choices become reversible, so sometimes it's worth filling a few potholes.

### 22.3 Barriers to Atomic Changes

Why not just commit everything at once?

- **Technical limitations**: VCS operations scale linearly with change size; large commits can exhaust memory or block other writers, making atomicity not just unwise but impossible. Sharding is the workaround, at the cost of complexity.
- **Merge conflicts**: probability grows with files touched and engineers working concurrently. Sneaking a whole-repo change in over a weekend works at small scale; at Google "somebody is always making changes to the repository."
- **No haunted graveyards**: SREs' mantra applied to code. A haunted graveyard is a business-critical system so ancient or complex that nobody dares change it, frozen under layers of defensive bureaucracy. These are anathema to LSCs because they block migrations, decommissioning, and upgrades. The counter is "good, ol'-fashioned testing": thoroughly tested software can be changed with confidence regardless of age.
- **Heterogeneity**: LSCs work only when computers do the bulk of the effort, and computers need consistent environments. Google standardizes what it can (common presubmit behavior) and asks teams to omit project-specific checks for LSC shards — most teams cooperate.
- **Testing**: CI runs not just immediately impacted tests but everything transitively depending on changed files — probably overkill, and the farther a test is from the change, the less likely a failure is the change's fault. Small independent shards are easier to validate (root-causing a failure among 25 files is straightforward; among 10,000 it's a needle in a haystack), at the cost of rerunning the same heavyweight tests repeatedly — a trade Google consciously makes because engineer time beats compute.

**Case study: testing LSCs and the TAP train.** LSCs account for 10–20% of changes in many projects. Even a one-line signature change becomes complex across a thousand sites, and a big LSC could trigger a rerun of every test at Google. The TAP (Test Automation Platform) train exploits two facts: LSCs rarely interact with each other, and most affected tests pass for most LSCs. Every three hours: (1) run a sample of 1,000 random tests per change; (2) combine passing changes into one uber-change, "the train"; (3) run the union of all directly affected tests (can be every test at Google, 6+ hours); (4) rerun each nonflaky failure against each individual change to find the culprit; (5) issue per-change reports as evidence of safety.

- **Code review**: even LSCs go through review, and sharding makes reviewing tractable.

**Case study: scoped_ptr to std::unique_ptr.** Google's pre-C++11 smart pointer had 500,000+ references when `std::unique_ptr` made it obsolete. Over several months, engineers migrated in parallel; at peak the process generated, tested, and committed 700+ changes touching 15,000+ files *per day* (10x that today). The long tail was pure Hyrum's Law: nuanced behavior dependencies, race conditions with other engineers' changes, uses inside generated code. Because `scoped_ptr` appeared as parameter types in widely used APIs, the endgame required a trick: make `scoped_ptr` a type alias of `std::unique_ptr`, textually substitute, then delete the alias.

### 22.4 LSC Infrastructure

The most important support, the authors argue, has been cultural rather than technical:

- **Policies and culture.** Monorepo openness means any engineer can edit any file — historically, the cost of generating and reviewing changes was roughly symmetric, which naturally limited scope. Cheap generation broke that symmetry: one engineer could impose review burden company-wide. So a lightweight approval process emerged, run by a committee of experienced engineers plus domain experts. Its goal is to help authors produce the best changes, not to prohibit — but it sometimes says a cleanup isn't worth it (e.g., fixing a common typo with no way to prevent recurrence). Local owners may question a commit but learned they don't hold a veto; a good FAQ plus a track record of improvements produced widespread endorsement.
- **Codebase insight.** Kythe provides semantic indexes ("where are the callers of this function?"), and compiler-based tools (ClangMR, JavacFlume, Refaster) do parallel AST transformations; smaller jobs can use sed/perl. The key requirement: human effort must scale *sublinearly* with the codebase. Rule of thumb: more than ~500 edits → learn the change-generation tools.
- **Change management.** Rosie shards a master change into independently testable, reviewable, submittable pieces — less a tool than "an entire platform for making LSCs at Google scale."
- **Testing.** Each shard is tested over the transitive closure of tests it might affect, not with standard project presubmits, since shards cut arbitrarily across projects.
- **Language support.** Type aliasing and forwarding functions let old and new coexist during migration (Go added such features specifically to support refactoring). Statically typed languages are far easier — compilers reject invalid transformations up front — while Python, Ruby, and JavaScript are "extra difficult for maintainers." Language choice is tied to code lifespan: productivity-focused languages are harder to maintain at scale. Automatic formatters (clang-format, google-java-format) are crucial so machine-generated changes "fit in" with human-written code — clang-format was originally motivated by exactly this.

**Case study: Operation RoseHub.** In 2017 the Mad Gadget vulnerability in Apache Commons allowed remote code execution (it was used to ransom the San Francisco transit agency). Volunteer Googlers used BigQuery to find affected GitHub projects and sent 2,600+ patches — an LSC executed by 50+ humans instead of automation.

### 22.5 The LSC Process

Four phases with nebulous boundaries — authorization, change creation, sharding and submitting, cleanup. Ideally they're anticipated *during* design: successor systems are built with automatic migration paths in mind.

- **Authorization.** Authors write a brief proposal (motivation, estimated shard count, FAQ, change description) and get domain review from API owners. A committee of about a dozen gives feedback — most commonly directing reviews to a single "global approver" instead of every local owner. The committee is liberal: it has rejected only dangerous changes (NULL → nullptr) or extremely low-value ones (British vs. American spelling), and its bar has dropped as LSC costs have fallen. It also serves as the escalation path for disputes, rarely needed.
- **Change creation.** Generate one comprehensive global change, keep regenerating it as users backslide or merge conflicts land. Changes should look as human-generated as possible — which is what makes style guides and formatters load-bearing.
- **Sharding and submitting.** Rosie shards by project boundaries and ownership, capping concurrent shards and running at low priority. Several themes:
  - *Cattle versus pets.* Handcrafted changes are pets — worked on for days or weeks, owned with pride. LSC shards are cattle: nameless, faceless commits that may be rolled back or rejected at any time with little cost, since tooling regenerates them cheaply.
  - *Testing.* Most shards affect fewer than 1,000 tests. High-impact shards are batched: run the union of affected tests once, then per-shard only the intersection with failures. At scale, low-probability events are certainties, so flaky tests are the enemy — to the point that **recently flaky tests are ignored when submitting LSC shards**: a machine-generated, semantics-preserving change is trusted more than a flaky test. Actual regressions caught this way are rare enough to handle by human communication.
  - *Mailing reviewers.* An owners-detection service weights OWNERS by expected ability to review the shard and adds reviewers when someone is unresponsive. Project-specific precommit checks that are irrelevant to an LSC are selectively disabled; preexisting failures are aggressively ignored — the social contract puts responsibility for preexisting failures on local owners.
  - *Reviewing.* In practice local owners give LSC shards only cursory review — they trust the infrastructure teams. Shards needing local context go to owners; the rest go to a global approver who uses pattern-based tooling to auto-approve conforming changes and manually inspects only anomalies.
  - *Submitting.* Thousands of changes per day. Decisions that used to be final — the name of a widely used symbol, a popular class's location — no longer need to be.
- **Cleanup.** "Done" varies from total removal to migrating only high-value references and letting the rest die organically (the systems you most want to decompose are, sadly, the most resilient — "the plastic six-pack rings of the code ecosystem"). In almost all cases you need to prevent reintroduction; Tricorder flags new uses of the deprecated API at review time, tying LSCs into Google's deprecation process.

### 22.6 Conclusion

LSCs expand design-time possibilities, let infrastructure teams migrate the codebase to new systems, language versions, and idioms — keeping it consistent "spatially and temporally" — and all with a few dozen engineers supporting tens of thousands. Any organization, regardless of size, should think about how it would make sweeping changes; the ability buys flexibility as you scale.

### 22.7 TL;DRs

- An LSC process makes it possible to rethink the immutability of certain technical decisions.
- Traditional models of refactoring break at large scales.
- Making LSCs means making a habit of making LSCs.

## Key terms

- **Large-scale change (LSC)**: a set of logically related changes that cannot practically be submitted as a single atomic unit.
- **Shard**: one independently testable/reviewable/submittable piece of an LSC, produced by splitting the master change along project and ownership boundaries.
- **Rosie**: Google's platform for sharding a master change and driving each shard through an independent test-mail-submit pipeline.
- **TAP / the TAP train**: Google's Test Automation Platform; the "train" batches many LSCs every three hours, sampling 1,000 random tests per change, then running the union of affected tests on the combined uber-change.
- **Global approver**: a reviewer with approval rights across the whole repository who reviews LSC shards with pattern-based tooling instead of every local owner reviewing their slice.
- **Haunted graveyard**: an ancient, complex, business-critical system nobody dares to change; its antidote is thorough testing.
- **Cattle vs. pets**: handcrafted changes are pets (nurtured, taken personally); LSC shards are cattle (disposable, regenerable at near-zero cost).
- **Kythe**: Google's semantic indexing tool, mapping callers, derivations, and cross-references codebase-wide.
- **Operation RoseHub**: Google's volunteer 2017 campaign sending 2,600+ GitHub patches for the Mad Gadget Apache Commons vulnerability.
