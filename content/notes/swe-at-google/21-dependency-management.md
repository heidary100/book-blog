> Dependency management — handling networks of libraries we don't control — adds time and scale complexity on top of source control: changes happen outside your organization, without visibility, so they can break you at any moment. The canonical hazard is the diamond dependency; the industry's default answer, SemVer, is only a lossy human estimate of change risk that SAT-solvers treat as absolute truth. The chapter surveys what doesn't work, argues that testing and CI provide real evidence where version numbers provide guesses, and warns that exporting dependencies (gflags, AppEngine) has hidden long-term costs too.

## The big idea

Source control and dependency management are separated by one question: "Does our organization control the development/update/management of this subproject?" Source control is the easy side — check things in, get them into the build, run the tests, don't break trunk. Dependency management is the hard side: upstream changes happen without coordination or visibility into your private code, so the odds of breakage are much higher. Hence the chapter's strongest single piece of advice: *all else being equal, prefer source-control problems over dependency-management problems*. Redefine "organization" broadly (whole company, monorepo) to convert dependency problems into source-control problems wherever you can.

The deep difficulty is that a dependency isn't a snapshot, it's a **contract over time**. Every node in your dependency network will eventually ship new versions, some forced (security bugs), and you must manage the cascade of upgrades across nodes you don't control. SemVer compresses each change into a maintainer's guess ("breaking / additive / safe") and package managers escalate those guesses into hard constraints — which produces both *dependency hell* (versions disallowed that would work) and *false compatibility* (versions allowed that break, per Hyrum's Law). The authors are candid that they have no proven solution at ecosystem scale — the chapter is largely a map of what fails and why — but their bet is that evidence (running downstream tests under CI) beats self-attestation (version numbers), a model they call Live at Head.

## Section by section

### 21.1 Why Is Dependency Management So Difficult?

The naive formulation — "how do we import a package" — is necessary but not sufficient. The real problem is managing a *network* of dependencies and their changes over time: some nodes are direct needs, many arrive transitively, all eventually need updating, and each update can cascade. Key sub-problems:

- **Conflicting requirements and diamond dependencies.** The central scenario: `libbase` is used by `liba` and `libb` (separate orgs), and both are used by your `libuser`. If `libbase` makes an incompatible change and `liba` upgrades while `libb` doesn't, there is *no general way* for `libuser` to assemble both. Languages tolerate this differently: Java has shading (symbol renaming); C++ has essentially zero tolerance — a diamond violates the One Definition Rule and invites undefined behavior. Even where embedding multiple versions works, it collapses once *types* flow between libraries: a map from libbase v1 can't be passed to an API from libbase v2. The only easy fix is jumping versions; otherwise you locally patch dependencies — extra hard because the person who discovers the conflict usually knows none of the projects involved. Downgrading may be impossible when the upgrade was security-forced. The whole problem reduces to: "How do we avoid conflicting requirements while still allowing change among noncoordinating groups?"
- **Importing dependencies and compatibility promises.** Reuse beats reimplementation, but a dependency's ongoing maintenance cost depends on what the provider promises. The spectrum: the C++ standard library offers near-indefinite API *and ABI* compatibility (see Standing Document 8 for what changes to expect); Go promises source compatibility only; Google's Abseil explicitly reserves the right to break ABI, promising only that any breaking API change ships with an automated refactoring tool — the worst case for users becomes "run this tool"; Boost promises nothing, as a deliberate experimental proving ground. None of this is about the maintainers' skill — it's about what the project prioritizes. These are software engineering concerns, not programming ones: violating SD-8 and relying on binary compat from Boost "works fine… so long as you never upgrade."
- **Considerations when importing.** For a programming project (today only), importing is nearly free. For a software engineering project, Google encourages a checklist before importing: does it have tests, do they pass, who provides it (reputation matters), what compatibility does it aspire to, how popular, how long will you depend on it, how often does it break, and internally — how hard to reimplement, who will do upgrades, what incentives exist to stay current? There's no perfect formula; "we fail at this ourselves, more often than not."
- **How Google handles importing.** "In short: we could do better." Most dependencies are internal (i.e., just source control, by design). External ones go into `third_party` with a light checklist: builds with our build system, isn't a duplicate, and two engineers sign up as OWNERS. The cautionary tale: Alice imports a package to demo before vacation; Bob co-owns it; neither needs implementation knowledge; usage (including transitive) grows invisibly until something deep in Search depends on it; years later a CVE forces an upgrade while Bob manages and Alice has changed teams. Nobody with practice, knowledge, or ownership does a costly multi-release upgrade under security-team pressure. Google's lesson: it needs a higher bar for ownership and to make regular updates easier and orphaned-but-critical packages harder — while acknowledging that telling engineers "no, you can't use the thing that solves your problem" is genuinely difficult.

### 21.2 Dependency Management, In Theory

A good solution avoids conflicting requirements (including diamonds) even in a dynamic ecosystem, without assuming version stability, limited fan-out, cross-organization coordination, or big compute; a *great* solution does all that. Four known options:

- **Nothing Changes (static dependency model).** Freeze everything; allow only fixes no user code could break. Maximally stable, and arguably the right model for young projects — most orgs don't know they're building something for decades. But over long periods it's false: there's no early-warning system for security bugs, and one forced upgrade can cascade through the whole network. Version selection: trivial — there are no versions.
- **Semantic Versioning.** The de facto standard: major.minor.patch where major = breaking, minor = additive, patch = low-risk. Requirements like "libbase ≥ 1.5" become edge labels on a dependency graph; finding a satisfying assignment of versions is a SAT problem (footnote: proven NP-complete). No solver promises a solution exists — a major bump at the bottom with partial adoption above recreates the diamond. When no assignment satisfies all constraints, that's "dependency hell."
- **Bundled distribution models.** An organization gathers a mutually compatible set and releases it as one unit — Linux distros, "the npm graph at time T." This converts the network into a single aggregated dependency and introduces *distributors*: engineers who find, patch, and test compatible sets. Version selection is delegated to them.
- **Live at Head.** The model Winters and colleagues advocate: the dependency-management extension of trunk-based development. Unpin everything, drop SemVer, and make providers test changes against the whole visible ecosystem before committing. Time and choice are removed: always depend on the current version. Unintentional breakage is caught by downstream CI and shouldn't be committed; forced breaks (security) wait for downstream updates or ship with an automated migration tool — essential for closed-source consumers, since it lets anyone upgrade without expert knowledge. Instead of *estimating* risk ("I think this is safe"), changes are tested experimentally: an efficiency-only change sails through; an observable API change causes hundreds of test failures the author must weigh — ideally unwinding brittle test assumptions and shipping refactoring tooling ahead of time. The model assumes ubiquitous tests/CI, providers bound by downstream breakage, and consumers keeping tests green (or being skipped as low-signal). It works best when source is visible; closed-source dependents remain the weak spot.

### 21.3 The Limitations of SemVer

Is a version number a promise or an estimate? It can't be a promise: compatibility with *source* alone is unprovable, and ill-behaved users can break on even "additive" changes (a polyfill defining the new API early; reflection counting the number of exported symbols). SemVer works as shorthand, but SAT-solvers built on it treat maintainer estimates as absolute — a shaky foundation that fails in both directions:

- **Overconstraining.** If `libbase` contains `Foo` and `Bar`, only `Foo` is used by dependents, and `Bar` is broken, SemVer forces a major bump — and solvers reject 2.x even though everything would work. The compression is lossy unless versioning happens at the granularity of individual atomic APIs (rare; the Node ecosystem's single-function packages are the exception). Much of "dependency hell" is wasted effort caused by exactly this.
- **Overpromising.** Changes that don't touch the API contract can still break users: a one-millisecond delay, changed log formats, changed import order, changed order of "unordered" results. This is Hyrum's Law in action — "patch" versions are observably different, which is why constraints like `>1.1.14` even exist. "Breaking change" is a misnomer: some theoretically-breaking changes are safe in practice (removing an unused API) and some "safe" changes break code. Whether a change is breaking can only be judged against actual usage — information absent from the SemVer formulation — so a solver can green-light combinations that yield build errors or runtime bugs of unbounded severity.
- **Motivations.** Maintainers bear a tiny fraction of a version bump's cost; most users are indirect and follow no release notes; and maintainer-users are incentivized to break (designing without legacy constraints is easier). Hence projects should publish explicit compatibility intent. Go and Clojure handle this neatly: a major bump means an entirely new package — "a certain sense of justice": pay for the nuclear option with repackaging. And bumps are human decisions; meaningful semantic changes can't be detected computationally, so at a thousand dependencies you should "expect some amount of chaos simply from human error."
- **Minimum Version Selection (MVS).** Russ Cox's 2018 Go-modules insight: when a requirement says "≥1.7," build with 1.7, not the newest available. The author almost certainly developed against 1.7, so that's the version with (anecdotal) interoperability evidence. Absent perfect prediction, take the smallest possible step forward — like committing an hour of work rather than a year. The jury is out on whether MVS makes SemVer good enough, but the authors call it "a manifest improvement."
- **So, does SemVer work?** Yes, at limited scale, provided providers are accurate and responsible, dependencies are fine-grained, and all usage stays within expected usage. At scale you can't keep any of those three true constantly; compounded fidelity loss dominates, showing up as both false positives and false negatives.

### 21.4 Dependency Management with Infinite Resources

A thought experiment: drop the compute constraint, keep only the visibility and coordination limits. SemVer dominates today for three reasons — it needs only local information, it assumes no tests/CI/compute, and it's the existing practice. But the local-information excuse is weak: dependency networks form only inside organizations or inside OSS (where source is visible), so downstream-usage information exists and is merely ignored. With enough compute, instead of a maintainer's estimate ("this change should be easy to adopt") you can produce evidence: run the tests of downstream dependencies against every proposed change and make a statistical argument for safety in the practical, Hyrum's-Law sense — "the tests still pass, the change is good," no classification needed. You can then prioritize testing (heavily used, well-maintained, high-signal projects; author-supplied risk estimates; sampling for risk mitigation vs. exhaustive testing for the strong guarantee) and map Chapter 12's four varieties of change onto proportionate testing levels. What's missing in the ecosystem: universal unit tests, an actually indexed dependency graph (most systems list dependencies but not *dependents*), available CI compute, unpinned dependencies for experimental testing, and using project history/reputation as evidence. Test only against current stable versions and this model converges to Live at Head — the open question is whether providers will accept the responsibility with realistic resources.

### 21.5 Exporting Dependencies

Publishing software is also a dependency-management decision, with two failure modes: reputation drag (the Apache saying: "community over code" — good code with poor community support still hurts) and an engineering-efficiency tax when internal and external copies drift ("given time, all forks will become expensive").

- **gflags (circa 2006).** Google open-sourced its C++ flags library and it went badly: with no large-scale refactoring capability it couldn't be moved internally; repository segregation rules and external-contribution licensing meant patches could never be reincorporated — the project was "doomed to be either a 'throw over the wall' release or a disconnected fork." Portability eroded as Google narrowed to one in-house toolchain; support was nobody's team priority; the project rotted and was forked externally. The lesson: "don't release things without a plan (and a mandate) to support it for the long term." The technical kicker: teams needing dual-internal/external builds discovered (by trial and error) the stable common subset between the forks — and when C++ library teams later tweaked observable-but-undocumented internals, those teams' builds broke, delaying an optimization worth thousands of aggregate CPUs. Not because updating 250 million lines was hard, but because a tiny handful of projects relied on unpromised behavior. Hyrum's Law applies even to forked APIs.
- **AppEngine.** In 2014 a Python runtime update was coupled to the C++ compiler/standard-library update, so upgrading one meant upgrading the other. Most projects were fine — but paying AppEngine customers couldn't or wouldn't move (32-bit vs. 64-bit resource costs), so the forced switch was delayed, freezing the *entire transitive closure* of C++ dependencies at the older compiler for almost three years. Every bug fix and optimization in that closure had to remain cross-compatible. Internal users live inside your visibility and constraints; external users — especially paying ones — impose competing priorities that make pure engineering trade-offs impossible.

### 21.6 Conclusion

Dependency management is inherently hard: it seeks stable management of complex dependency webs with no coordination assumed. SemVer summarizes perceived risk in a lossy way and presupposes that severity is predictable without knowing how an API is consumed — Hyrum's Law says otherwise. It works at small scale, better with MVS, and poorly as networks grow. The plausible future: replace maintainer estimates with experience-driven evidence (running downstream tests), with providers taking responsibility for their consumers.

### 21.7 TL;DRs

- Prefer source-control problems over dependency-management problems; broader organizations (monorepos) are a simplifying trade.
- Adding a dependency isn't free for a software engineering project; import deliberately, understanding ongoing support costs.
- A dependency is a contract: providers and consumers both have rights and responsibilities, and providers should state clearly what they promise over time.
- SemVer is a lossy shorthand for "how risky does a human think this change is?"; SAT-solvers escalate estimates to absolutes, producing both overconstraint (dependency hell) and underconstraint (versions that shouldn't work but are allowed).
- Testing and CI provide actual evidence that a set of versions works together.
- Minimum-version strategies improve fidelity by selecting what the author actually tested against.
- Ubiquitous tests, CI, and cheap compute could transform dependency management — but only with a fundamental change in how providers and consumers think about responsibility.
- Providing a dependency isn't free either: unsupported releases cost reputation and fork cost; supporting *with* stability limits your choices; supporting without stability invites Hyrum's Law dependence from important outsiders.

## Key terms

- **Diamond dependency**: the canonical conflicting-requirements problem — one low-level dependency needed in two incompatible versions because two mid-level dependencies upgraded at different times.
- **Compatibility promise**: a provider's explicit statement of what it guarantees across releases (API, ABI, supported usage, support duration) — e.g., C++ SD-8, Go's source compatibility, Abseil's "breaking changes ship with a refactoring tool," Boost's no-promise.
- **Shading**: Java's mechanism for renaming an embedded dependency's symbols so multiple versions can coexist in one build.
- **SemVer**: major.minor.patch versioning where the numbers encode a maintainer's estimate of change risk; "a lossy-compression shorthand."
- **Dependency hell**: a state where no assignment of dependency versions satisfies all declared SemVer constraints.
- **Minimum Version Selection (MVS)**: Russ Cox's strategy of selecting the *minimum* versions satisfying each requirement, staying closest to what the author actually built and tested against.
- **Live at Head**: always depending on the current version of everything, with providers testing changes against the visible ecosystem before commit and shipping migration tooling for unavoidable breaks.
- **Bundled distribution model**: finding a mutually compatible set of dependencies and releasing it as one versioned unit (Linux distros); the maintainers of the parts are replaced by *distributors*.
- **third_party / OWNERS**: Google's monorepo location and ownership model for external dependencies; the Alice-and-Bob story shows its failure mode of orphaned-but-critical packages.
